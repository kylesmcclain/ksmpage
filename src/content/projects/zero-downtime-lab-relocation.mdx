## The shape of the risk

Everything that could delay this project was owned by somebody else. Circuit activation dates
come from a carrier. Termination work comes from a cabling crew. Equipment arrives when the movers
say it arrives. The one thing fully under my control was whether the design was finished and
correct before any of those dependencies landed — so that was where the effort went.

## Approach

**Design the network before the building is ready.** The site's topology was designed around
Fortinet firewalling and switching well ahead of the move, with every required network drop mapped
against the intended floor layout and the physical placement of each instrument. Nothing was
improvised on site.

**Sequence the vendors, do not queue behind them.** Structured cabling and ISP work were ordered
and scheduled so that circuit activation and physical termination both completed _before_ equipment
arrived. The most common way a move like this fails is discovering on Saturday that the carrier's
date was Monday.

**Own the physical move.** Relocation and rack-up of infrastructure were handled directly rather
than delegated, which kept the person who designed the topology in the room while it was being
assembled.

**Validate service by service.** Connectivity and instrument reachability were confirmed
individually before the site was released — not with a single ping test, but against the actual
list of what the lab needed to be able to reach on Monday.

## Result

Laboratory and R&D operations resumed Monday with zero downtime and no emergency remediation.

The relocation is the clearest example I have of a principle I hold generally: a cutover with no
rollback window is survivable only if the failure path was planned while there was still time to
plan it.
